The idea

One instrument to build the riddim, and play the desk.

TowerDub started from a simple wish: a single, tactile instrument where you program a reggae riddim and then perform the dub, the way a mix was once played on the desk, in one live take.

It is a groovebox in the compact sense: one rack that shows every instrument and its mixer controls at a glance, and one editor per instrument for the details. The sound and the workflow follow the dub tradition: cut-outs, echo throws, filter sweeps and a siren, all played live and recorded as they happen.

Roots, warm, never bright

Sine-based low end, soft saturation, dark filter ceilings and a delay that wears down each repeat. The aim is a worn, muted sound, not a shiny one.

Built for touch

Knobs are dragged horizontally so the page can still scroll. The keyboard maps to scale degrees, not to a piano. Everything is made for fingers on glass.

Everything in one rack

Nine instruments, five effect blocks, one transport. Volume, delay send, reverb send and a CUT button sit on every line.

How it plays

From a blank grid to an exported dubplate.

  1. Build the riddim

    Place steps on a 16th-note grid for four drum voices, and notes for bass, chords, lead, pads and a sampler. Each instrument has four patterns and its own length, from 16 to 128 steps.

  2. Shape the sound

    Every instrument has five sound controls, and a long press on a step lets those controls change that step alone (parameter locks). Each step can also carry a trigger probability.

  3. Send it into the space

    Each line has its own delay send and reverb send, feeding a tape-style echo and a dark reverb shared by the whole rack.

  4. Dub it live

    Hit CUT on a line, route instruments through the resonant Dub Filter, throw echoes, freeze the delay, play the siren. The mix is the performance.

  5. Record and export

    Dub Record captures the whole stereo mix to a WAV file, ready to share from the system share sheet.

Nine instruments, five sound controls each.

Synthesis

How each voice is made.

All instrument voices are custom synthesis written for the app. Only the Delay and Reverb use Apple's built-in audio units. Several instruments have a Morph knob: one macro that moves the timbre across several keyframes, designed so that no position sounds better just because it is louder.

Drums

Kick

A sine from 27 to 110 Hz with an exponential decay of 40 to 390 ms. Punch adds a downward pitch glide and, in its upper half, a very short high-passed noise click. Tone adds second and third harmonics and a bell boost at 150 Hz. Drive is tanh saturation blended with the dry signal; the click is added after it, so it stays sharp.

Snare

A sine body (140 to 320 Hz) plus white noise band-passed around 1.6 to 2.4 kHz. Snap sets how fast the noise comes in, and a small rimshot-style click is always present.

Hats

White noise through a 6 to 10 kHz high-pass. Grain adds metallic partials above its midpoint (inharmonic ratios) and a gated shaker texture below it. Choke shortens the length of the hat.

Perc

Modal synthesis: four inharmonic sine modes on a base between 90 and 400 Hz. Morph slides the mode ratios from a tom to a bell. Resonance sets the size and tail of a shell resonator, and a short noise transient gives the attack.

Synths

Bass

The reference sound is a pure sine. Morph moves from a sine with a sub-octave, through the pure sine at the centre, to a triangle with a trace of saw. Drive saturates before a two-pole low-pass (80 to 1,600 Hz, logarithmic). The filter cutoff follows each note's envelope: it opens on the attack and settles back, which gives the classic dub "wow". Sequenced notes end smoothly just before the next note starts, like a monophonic bass line.

Chords

Each key plays a diatonic triad (root, third, fifth) from the active scale, so a chord always agrees with the bass. Warmth is tanh saturation applied to each of the three voices before they are summed, which avoids the muddy intermodulation of saturating a whole chord. Spread detunes the third and fifth by up to 35 cents. Sweep is a resonant low-pass that opens on each hit and closes again with the chord. Morph runs from sine, through triangle, to saw and pulse.

Lead

A held, breathing voice. Below the centre of Morph, drawbar organ harmonics with a hint of preamp grain; at the centre, a pure sine; above it, a triangle moving to a saw with a formant resonance and breath noise that follows the note. Filter covers roughly 220 Hz to 3 kHz, Width detunes left and right oscillators by up to 18 cents, and Attack and Decay shape a slow swell and a soft release.

Pads

A bed of low-passed noise (Grain sets how dark it is) under a tonal layer built from detuned voice pairs (Swarm). A slow LFO makes the filter breathe, and Morph also adds a very slow pitch drift of a few cents. The envelope is long, about two seconds.

Sampler

Record a sound with the microphone (tap REC, tap again to stop, up to 8 seconds), trim the start and end on a waveform, then place it on the grid. Four slots, each with its own patterns. Pitch is varispeed over plus or minus one octave. Tape is a one-knob macro: towards the left, a darker, worn tape with saturation and gentle wow and flutter; towards the right, a thin "radio" band.

Sequencer

Sixteenth-note steps, with room to breathe.

  • Length and patterns. 16, 32, 64 or 128 steps per pattern, set independently for each instrument, with four patterns per instrument. Changing pattern while playing is queued so it lands cleanly: at the next bar for the drums, at the end of the pattern for the synths.
  • Velocity. Three levels on the drums (Strong, Medium, Ghost). On the keyboards, velocity comes from where the finger lands on the key.
  • Parameter locks. Long-press a step and the instrument's knobs edit only that step. Available on drums, Bass, Chords and the Sampler.
  • Trigger probability. 100, 75, 50 or 25 percent per step, drawn on every pass of the loop.
  • Groove. Swing from 0 to 50 percent delays only the off-beat sixteenths. Tempo runs 40 to 200 BPM; a new project starts at 68, a one-drop feel.
  • Scales. Natural minor, Dorian, Mixolydian, minor pentatonic and chromatic. The scale is shared by all melodic instruments, so everything stays in key. The keyboard has 12 keys on two rows and shifts by whole scale octaves.
  • Live recording. REC overdubs notes quantized to the nearest step. The real length of each note is captured when the key is released.
  • Editing. Copy and paste a pattern, CLEAR with a RESTORE safety net.
Effects & dub tools

The desk, played live.

  • CUT. A push-button on every line. Changes are ramped over about 16 ms so a cut never clicks.
  • Delay. Time up to 1.5 seconds, Feedback up to 100 percent, Mix, and SYNC buttons that set the time from the tempo (quarter, dotted eighth, eighth, dotted sixteenth, sixteenth). Age darkens every repeat through a low-pass inside the loop, from 12 kHz down to 1.5 kHz, for a worn-tape character. FREEZE holds the echo by closing the delay's input while you press.
  • Reverb. Space steps through six spaces, from plate to cathedral, and the old tail is cross-faded rather than cut when you change it. Pre sets 0 to 150 ms of pre-delay, Tone darkens it and adds low-end weight. It is meant to sound dark and bass-resonant, not hi-fi.
  • Dub Filter. A resonant filter in the King Tubby tradition. One big Cutoff knob: centre is neutral, left closes the low-pass, right lifts the high-pass. Res narrows the band. Eight switches choose which instruments go through it, so you can sweep the drums and leave the bass alone.
  • Dub Siren. A sine carrier (150 to 900 Hz) modulated by an LFO, with Tune, Rate and Mode. Hold the pad to sound it; it has its own volume, delay and reverb sends.
  • Mic input. A live pass-through with its own sends, to run a voice or a field sound through the echo.
  • Master. Everything meets in a peak limiter before the output.
Recording & projects

Capture the take, keep the versions.

  • Dub Record. Taps the master output after the limiter, so you capture exactly what you hear: instruments, effects returns, siren and mic. The file is an uncompressed WAV, renamed, deleted or exported from the project menu with a swipe.
  • Projects and mixes. A project holds the riddim (patterns, sound settings, tempo, swing, scale) and a list of Mix versions: effect settings, line levels, cuts, sends and filter switches. You can keep several dubs of the same riddim and switch between them without touching the arrangement.
  • Autosave. Every five seconds and when the app goes to the background. A Demo Project is loaded on first launch so there is sound straight away.
Guided tutorial

Learn it by doing it.

Version 1.1 adds an interactive tutorial for people who have never used a music-making app. A welcome screen offers to start or skip it. Each step puts a ring on the real control and waits for you to do the gesture, then moves on.

It builds a riddim step by step in a throwaway project: drums, chords, bass, a live-recorded melody, a recorded sample, delay and reverb, then a live dub that is recorded and exported. If you finish at least one section, the result is kept as "My First Riddim". It can be replayed any time from the project menu.

Under the hood

How it keeps time.

  • Engine. One audio engine at 44.1 kHz stereo. Each of the nine instruments has its own mixer, feeding the main mix and the delay, reverb and filter chains in parallel.
  • Clock. A look-ahead scheduler ticks every 20 ms and places everything due in the next 100 ms at an absolute audio time, rather than relying on a loose timer.
  • Two playback paths. Drums, Chords, sequenced Bass and the Sampler are rendered for every hit and placed to the sample. Lead, Pads and live-played Bass are real-time voices, started by a timer.
  • Built with Swift, SwiftUI and AVAudioEngine. Delay and Reverb use Apple's audio units; all instrument and siren voices are custom DSP.

Scope of version 1.1

TowerDub is self-contained: there is no MIDI, no AUv3 plug-in mode and no import of external audio files. Samples are recorded inside the app.
